## Strategic recommendation

This is a strong strategy, and the deeper research supports the core idea: the most valuable thing ROMAS can build first is not only software, but a trusted intelligence layer for radiation oncology. Imaging Wire’s current model is already a meaningful proof point. Its subscription page positions the product as simplified radiology news for busy professionals, claims an audience of more than 37,000 imaging leaders, and its site structure explicitly includes Top Stories, Newsletters, Shows, Sponsorship, adjacent vertical newsletters, and a referral program. In other words, it is not just a publication; it is an audience, monetization, and category-building engine. citeturn37view0turn36view0turn36view1turn36view2

For ROMAS, the opportunity is arguably better because radiation oncology is narrower, more clinically specialized, and less crowded from a media standpoint, while the AI, workflow, reimbursement, and guideline landscape is moving quickly. ASTRO is actively organizing around responsible clinical AI use, and a joint ESTRO-AAPM statement already frames AI in radiotherapy as something that requires validation, commissioning, quality assurance, training, and monitoring rather than casual adoption. That means there is real demand for trusted interpretation, not just link aggregation. citeturn13search1turn13search7turn13search3

My recommendation is to build this as an umbrella product with two layers. The umbrella should be **ROMAS Intelligence**. The flagship media product should be **ROMAS Daily** or **ROMAS Wire**. Under that umbrella, the company can launch a weekday brief, a weekly papers digest, a short podcast, and later segmented feeds for physicists, physicians, dosimetrists, therapists, residents, and industry. The most important design decision is that the system should be **autonomous in discovery, scoring, drafting, and repackaging**, but **human-reviewed for publication at launch**. In a clinical niche, full “publish without review” autonomy is the wrong first move.

## What Imaging Wire proves and what ROMAS should copy

The research and your screenshots point to a simple but effective formula. Imaging Wire uses a highly skimmable format, repeated subscription prompts, a strong homepage hierarchy, partner newsletter cross-promotion, referral loops, and sponsor inventory. Its referral page literally markets the product as a free, twice-weekly, five-minute radiology email, while its signup flow asks for professional profile fields such as company and job title and bundles optional access to sponsored resources and partner communications. That combination is important: it converts anonymous traffic into known professionals, enriches audience data, and creates future monetization and segmentation leverage. citeturn36view2turn36view1turn36view0

ROMAS should copy the mechanics, but not the ceiling. Your screenshots show the right visual pattern already: one dominant hero story, compact cards with category and date, inline sponsor placements, and repeated signup friction points. ROMAS should keep that exact interaction model because clinicians skim on mobile. The difference is editorial posture. Imaging Wire is primarily curation and industry journalism; ROMAS should position itself as **clinical intelligence for modern radiation oncology**. That means each item should answer four questions quickly: what happened, why it matters, who it affects, and what ROMAS thinks it means for clinical operations, AI adoption, or business strategy.

That leads to the right content architecture. The weekday brief should stay short: one hero item, two to four major developments, one paper highlight, and a brief ROMAS Insight. The deeper literature work should move to a weekly issue because that is where credibility will compound. Imaging Wire shows that brevity drives habit; ROMAS should preserve that brevity for the daily product while reserving higher-effort paper critique for a separate weekly layer. citeturn36view2turn37view0

## The agent blueprint

The literature agent should use PubMed as the primary discovery backbone through NCBI’s E-utilities, with supporting enrichment from Crossref and arXiv. PubMed gives you stable programmatic search across the clinical literature; Crossref is useful for metadata completion and, importantly, links into parts of the research ecosystem such as funding records and post-publication updates from trusted sources; arXiv is useful for early AI and technical work that has not yet landed in journals. The literature agent should score each paper on clinical workflow relevance, disease-site relevance, evidence level, AI relevance, and novelty, then create two outputs: a short daily abstract for the general brief and a deeper structured critique for the weekly papers issue. It should never publish an item based only on a title; require at least an abstract and preferably full-text access before final drafting. citeturn2search0turn2search3turn2search2turn13search3

The conference agent should monitor official abstract and meeting portals from ASTRO, AAPM, ESTRO, and ASCO. ASTRO’s annual meeting abstracts are published through an official abstracts resource, and both ASTRO and ASCO enforce explicit embargo policies around accepted abstracts and presentations. That means the conference agent needs an “embargo-aware” object model with a hard publish lock until the embargo lifts. This is non-negotiable. The conference agent’s value is highest when it converts large meetings into short, same-day clinical takeaways: what matters now for adaptive therapy, contouring, QA, planning automation, FLASH, proton therapy, and AI validation. citeturn6search0turn6search2turn6search3turn17search15turn17search16

The FDA agent should combine three layers: the FDA AI-enabled medical devices list for landscape awareness, openFDA device endpoints for discovery automation, and the official FDA device databases and 510(k) records for verification. FDA’s own API documentation explicitly says openFDA data is not validated for clinical or production use, so the right pattern is discovery through openFDA and publication only after verification against the official record. For ROMAS, every clearance should be normalized into product code, regulatory pathway, vendor, stated indication, modality relevance, radiation-oncology adjacency, and whether it appears to affect contouring, imaging guidance, planning, delivery, QA, or workflow. citeturn3search5turn3search10turn3search13turn3search14

The AI startup tracker should not depend on one commercial data vendor. The best base layer is public-source detection: company press rooms, SEC EDGAR APIs for listed companies, NIH RePORTER for grant activity, ClinicalTrials.gov for study activity, FDA clearances for market movement, and the company’s own site for hiring, product, and partnership announcements. Crunchbase’s API is real and useful, but its official documentation makes clear that access depends on API packages and custom pricing, so it should be treated as an optional enrichment layer rather than your core source of truth. In practice, this agent should classify events into funding, product release, regulatory, pilot, partnership, hiring signal, reimbursement signal, and clinical evidence signal. citeturn7search0turn7search1turn7search2turn8search3turn8search5

The reimbursement tracker should watch CMS’s Physician Fee Schedule tools and data, the Medicare Coverage Database, the downloadable LCD data, MLN Connects, and the Federal Register API. CMS’s own tools now expose searchable fee-schedule data, while the MCD contains NCDs, LCDs, draft articles, and related coverage materials, and the LCD download set is updated on a weekly cadence. The Federal Register API is useful for alerting, but its documentation is explicit that it is an informational API and not the official legal edition, so high-stakes summaries should verify against the linked official PDF or govinfo record. The new CMS-FDA RAPID pathway is exactly the kind of development this agent should catch because it links regulatory and coverage timing for breakthrough devices. citeturn4search0turn4search14turn4search13turn18search1turn18search3turn19search0turn19search3

The guideline tracker should monitor ASTRO guidelines, AAPM reports and task groups, ACR Appropriateness Criteria, and ESTRO guideline resources. This agent should not be just a page watcher; it should classify each change as new, revised, endorsed, reaffirmed, retired, or superseded, then map that change to audience segments and clinical workflow consequences. The most important editorial layer here is “practice delta”: what changed, what did not change, and whether the update should alter planning, contouring, QA, patient selection, toxicity management, or documentation. The existence of ASTRO’s AI Task Force and the joint ESTRO-AAPM AI guidance are strong signals that ROMAS should treat guideline and governance tracking as a first-class product, not an afterthought. citeturn5search0turn5search2turn5search3turn5search8turn13search1turn13search7

The vendor intelligence agent should watch official company newsrooms and investor channels for the core radiation oncology ecosystem: Elekta, RaySearch, Varian, Radformation, and adjacent players such as GE Healthcare or Siemens Healthineers where relevant. This agent should not only aggregate press releases; it should connect them to objective signals from the other agents. In practice that means every vendor event should be matched to any related FDA clearance, CMS development, trial record, abstract, guideline mention, or hiring spike. Working this way turns press releases into intelligence rather than marketing copy. The public official newsrooms needed for this are clearly available today for Elekta and RaySearch, and vendor announcements from Varian or Siemens Healthineers are already flowing through official press channels. citeturn9search1turn9search2turn9search0turn9search5

## Publishing, trust, and compliance

This product will only work if it is visibly more trustworthy than generic AI summarization. The editorial rule should be simple: **every public item gets source attribution, every interpretation is labeled as interpretation, and every clinically weighty claim gets a citation trail in the internal editorial workspace**. The daily public output can stay short, but internally you should preserve claim-level evidence objects so an editor can verify what the model actually used. That is especially important in a field where ASTRO and AAPM are emphasizing responsible evaluation, commissioning, monitoring, and training around AI use. citeturn13search1turn13search7

Operationally, the approval system should have three states. First, **autonomous discovery**, where agents ingest and rank. Second, **assisted drafting**, where the model prepares structured summaries, clinical implications, audience labels, and podcast scripts. Third, **human release**, where a reviewer checks the item, approves publication, or sends it back for revision. Over time you can allow low-risk categories such as vendor event roundups or conference logistics to publish with spot checks, but literature, reimbursement, guideline, and FDA summaries should remain reviewed until correction rates are clearly low.

Email compliance and deliverability need to be designed before your first send, not after. The FTC’s CAN-SPAM guidance still applies to commercial email, and Google’s current sender rules are especially important if you may send 5,000 or more messages per day to Gmail users. Google explicitly requires one-click unsubscribe for marketing and subscribed messages at that scale, requires alignment of the From domain with either SPF or DKIM for direct mail, recommends gradual ramp-up of volume, and says bulk senders should keep spam rates below 0.10% while avoiding 0.30% or higher. Beehiiv also documents that for custom domains it handles SPF and DKIM through CNAME-based setup, while DMARC must be configured for the custom sending domain. citeturn11search0turn28view1turn28view2turn28view3turn27search0turn27search11

Because you already have large professional email lists, the safest practical move is **not** to import and blast the whole database as an active marketing list on day one. A staged re-permission or soft-invite approach is better for reputation and compliance, especially if your audience includes EU or UK contacts, where regulators have detailed guidance on obtaining, recording, and managing consent. In other words, your advantages in distribution are real, but sender reputation is fragile and should be treated like infrastructure. citeturn11search1turn11search5turn28view2turn12search18

## Launching next week

The fastest credible launch is a **semi-autonomous MVP** with a human editorial checkpoint. The right goal for next week is not “all seven agents running perfectly.” The right goal is “a working publication with reliable inputs, clear editorial standards, enough automation to save time, and a repeatable morning publishing routine.”

On the first day, choose the public brand architecture, set the publishing domain, configure the newsletter platform, authenticate the domain, and put the subscribe experience onto the current ROMAS site with at least three capture points: a modal, an inline form, and a footer CTA. Imaging Wire’s own site uses repeated subscription prompts and professional-profile capture for a reason, and Beehiiv now supports embedded forms and configurable signup flows that can route new readers into surveys, recommendations, or thank-you pages. citeturn36view1turn35search0turn35search1turn35search7

On the second day, stand up the agent backbone in the simplest possible form. Build the canonical content database, wire the literature, FDA, conference, guideline, reimbursement, startup, and vendor ingestion jobs, and create a scoring rubric that outputs a publish queue rather than direct publication. Also stand up a private editorial view that lets you approve, reject, or edit items before they go live.

On the third day, generate the first three to five mock issues and the first podcast scripts. If you use Beehiiv’s native podcasting, confirm that your plan level matches your planned episode volume; otherwise use a separate host. If you plan to distribute broadly, Apple requires an Apple Podcasts Connect account and podcast RSS compliance, and Spotify for Creators can host and distribute podcasts for free if you prefer that route. Keep the podcast short and use the same item set as the email to avoid doubling editorial workload. citeturn30view0turn16search3turn16search13turn22search3turn22search7

On the fourth day, run a soft launch to a small internal or friendly pilot list. Watch deliverability, unsubscribes, spam complaints, and the editorial defect rate. The quality threshold should be simple: no hallucinated facts, no unlabeled opinion, no paper summaries without adequate evidence access, and no conference items that risk embargo violations.

On the fifth day, launch externally. My recommendation is that the public launch product set should be: a weekday email brief, a matching website archive, a 5-to-8 minute audio briefing, and a weekly “Top Papers” issue that is deeper and more technical. That is enough to look substantial, credible, and differentiated without overextending the team in week one.

For staffing, the minimum viable launch team is small: one engineer who can build the pipeline and editorial admin, one clinician-domain editor who can approve content quickly, and one person who can handle design, publishing ops, and audience management. If that must all be you for the first week, then reduce scope rather than reduce quality.
